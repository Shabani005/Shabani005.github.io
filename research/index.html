A study on the feasibility of using decision models to offload reasoning, and so lower inference cost

Reasoning models spend most of their tokens on questions they would have answered correctly anyway. Here a decision model — a small external model that returns a calibrated probability instead of text — is asked, per question, whether the cheap answer is good enough. Reasoning is bought only when it changes the outcome: 3.1× fewer tokens at 95% of the reasoning model's accuracy, and 21% fewer tokens at no measurable accuracy loss at all.

Why it matters

Reasoning is the most expensive thing a model does, and most of it is wasted

Turning reasoning on multiplies this model's token bill by 5.4× and buys 10.6 points of accuracy. It is charged on every question — including the 78% the model already answers correctly in one shot. The gate's only job is to find out which question is which, for a fraction of a cent, before the expensive tokens are spent.

3.0 billion tokens saved per million questions

3,033 fewer tokens on every question, at 95% of full-reasoning accuracy. The decision model that made those calls costs about $68 at that volume — roughly a thousandth of what the tokens it saved would have cost.

Nothing to train

No fine-tuning, no distillation, no change to the model or the prompt format. One extra call per question and a threshold. It sits in front of a model you already pay for, and you can take it out again in a line of code.

Works on any endpoint

The policy only needs a chat completion and a probability. Hosted or local, open or closed weights — the same 215-line runner drives all of them, which is how the result was replicated on a second model.

Result

The gate sits above the frontier you can already buy

Anyone can trade accuracy for cost by sending a share of questions to the reasoning model and answering the rest cheaply. That mix is the honest baseline — the dashed line. A policy is only worth anything if it lands above it.

Mechanism

Ask something else whether the answer is good

  1. Answer cheaply. The same model replies with reasoning disabled — a single short pass.
  2. Score it externally. A decision model is asked one question: is this answer correct? It returns a probability, not prose. Asking the model to grade itself does not work; an independent scorer does (area under the ROC curve 0.88 against 0.70 for self-assessment, measured in the source project).
  3. Escalate only on doubt. Below 0.7, either pay for the reasoning pass (gate + reasoning) or take another cheap attempt and keep the best-scored answer (gate only, which never reasons at all).
  4. Pay almost nothing for the decision. 1.27 calls per question, about $0.07 per thousand questions — three orders of magnitude below the reasoning tokens it replaces.

Two operating points

Same gate, two budgets

Keep the accuracy, pay 21% less

Gate + reasoning reaches 87.6% against 88.5% for always reasoning. The paired test cannot separate them (-0.9 points, 22 wins to 28 losses, McNemar p = 0.48) while spending 3,522 tokens instead of 4,465.

Never reason at all, 3.1× cheaper

Gate only retries cheaply and submits the best-scored answer. It beats one-shot answering by +6.4 points (p < 0.001) for 1.7× its tokens, and still costs 68% less than reasoning — 1,701 tokens per correct answer against 5,047.

By benchmark

Where the saving comes from

The gate gives up most on the hardest set, where the cheap answer is rarely right and the escalation is what carries it; on knowledge and routine reasoning it matches full reasoning at a fraction of the cost.

Limits

Where this does not hold

Two results here go against the idea, and both are in the released data. They bound what the headline number means: this is what a gate bolted onto a finished model can do, not what the approach is worth in principle — a distinction the next two sections take seriously.

Final answers, not agent steps

The gate is built to judge a finished answer. Applied to each move of an episode it is the wrong shape, as Figure 4 shows, and the veto variant that partly recovers is a different policy rather than a tuning of this one.

It only pays if the model overthinks

The saving tracks the gap between one-shot and reasoning accuracy. Replicated on a local 35B model whose one-shot answers already matched its reasoning, the gate cut tokens but bought no accuracy. Where reasoning is not wasted, there is nothing to reclaim.

One model family, one threshold

The headline run is a single model; the threshold (0.7) was picked on a separate development set and held fixed. A cross-validated sweep put the best global threshold within two points of it, but it is one knob chosen once, not a tuned-per-task policy.

Interpretation

Every gap left is a training gap

The model in this study was pretrained and post-trained to reason alone. It has no way to say "I am unsure", no habit of stopping early because something else will check it, and no experience of being told its answer was rejected. The gate has to work around all three from the outside, with a prompt. Three measurements point at that, rather than at the gate, as what is holding the numbers down:

  1. The judge is not the bottleneck. The decision model separates correct from incorrect answers at an area under the ROC curve of 0.88 — against 0.70 when the same model is asked to grade itself. It knows which answers are wrong. The loss comes after that, in what the model does about it.
  2. The model cannot act on a rejection. Told its answer was judged unlikely and asked for a different one, it mostly produces a variation of the same reasoning. In the multi-step replay the gate pushed the model off a good move 47 times and rescued it 18 — the veto signal arrives, and the model has no trained response to it.
  3. The right answer is often never proposed. An audit of the first version's failures found all eleven were cases where the correct answer appeared in none of the model's attempts. A gate can only pick among the candidates it is handed; producing a genuinely different one on request is a trained behaviour this model does not have.

So the headline numbers are what a gate can do despite the model, not with it. Whether training closes the gap is the obvious next experiment, and this study does not answer it — it sets the baseline that experiment would have to beat.

Next step

Post-train a model to use the gate, then run this benchmark again

The data needed to try it already exists: this run produced graded records for every question and policy, each carrying the cheap answer, the decision model's probability, whether that answer was right, and what happened after a rejection. That is a supervised signal for three behaviours the model currently lacks.

1. Teach it to defer

Train on (question, answer, was-it-correct) so the model emits its own stop-or-escalate signal instead of being judged from outside on every question. The decision model becomes the teacher, not a permanent tax on inference.

2. Teach it to revise

Train on the rejection traces — the cases where a second attempt after a low score did and did not recover the answer — so a veto produces a genuinely different approach rather than the same chain of thought in new words.

3. Re-run this benchmark unchanged

Same held-out questions, same four policies, same three numbers: accuracy against full reasoning, tokens per question, and the gain over an equal-budget traffic split. If the training hypothesis is right, the gap closes; if it does not, the hypothesis is wrong and the ceiling is real.
